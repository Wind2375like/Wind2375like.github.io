5. Selecting Input Probability Distributions

How to choose the input distributions a simulation feeds on: assess sample independence, hypothesize a distribution family, estimate its parameters by maximum likelihood, and judge the fit with chi-square and K-S tests.

In practice, an input distribution is specified based on data collected from the actual process by observing an input r.v. of interest.

Three approaches

  1. Use the actual data directly (e.g. customer service times): store the data in a “lookup” table and read the values in order during the simulation (trace-driven simulation).
    1. Disadvantage: this approach is very expensive and time-consuming (a large amount of data must be stored and read).
    2. Disadvantage: it can only reproduce what has happened historically.
    3. Use case: typically used in failure analysis, and to validate a model of an existing system (feed the model the same historical inputs and compare its output with the real system’s).
  2. Use the data to fit an empirical distribution (frequency distribution, histogram), and then sample from the empirical distribution during the simulation.
    1. Disadvantage: it is not possible to generate values outside the range of the observed data X1,…,XnX_1, \dots, X_nX1​,…,Xn​, i.e. outside [min⁡iXi, max⁡iXi]\left[\min_i X_i,\ \max_i X_i\right][mini​Xi​, maxi​Xi​].
  3. Use the data to fit a theoretical distribution (a parameterized distribution such as Exp(λ)\text{Exp}(\lambda)Exp(λ), N(μ,σ2)N(\mu, \sigma^2)N(μ,σ2), U(a,b)U(a, b)U(a,b)); use statistical methods to determine the quality of fit.
    1. Advantage: compact representation of the data (a few parameters instead of nnn numbers).
    2. Advantage: flexible → “what-if” questions (e.g. what if service becomes 10% faster? Just change a parameter).
    3. Advantage: it can generate values outside the observed range, and it smooths out the irregularities of a small sample.

Use the third approach whenever possible, i.e. whenever some theoretical distribution fits the data well.

Assumption: to fit a distribution, the data should be i.i.d. observations, i.e. X1,X2,…,XnX_1, X_2, \dots, X_nX1​,X2​,…,Xn​ are i.i.d. from an underlying distribution. (§5.1 checks the independence part.)

5.1. Techniques for assessing sample independence

Given X1,X2,…,XnX_1, X_2, \dots, X_nX1​,X2​,…,Xn​, in the order they were observed.

  • Runs test

The runs-up test of Chapter 3 (§3.4.3). It uses only the order of the observations (which one is larger than which), so it works for any continuous data, not just U(0,1)U(0,1)U(0,1) numbers.

Three steps to fit a theoretical distribution

  1. Hypothesize a family of distributions (§5.4).
  2. Estimate the parameters of the distribution (maximum likelihood estimator, MLE).
  3. Determine the quality of fit (validation).

5.2. Review of distributions and their parameters

The types of parameters:

  • Location parameter γ\gammaγ

It determines the location of the distribution (where it sits on the xxx-axis). Changing γ\gammaγ shifts the distribution but does not change its shape.

5.4. Step I: Hypothesize a distribution family

  • Prior knowledge of the random variable of interest
    • Service times should never be fitted using a normal distribution: a normal r.v. can be negative, a service time cannot (and service times are usually right-skewed, while the normal is symmetric).
    • Customers arrive at a bank one at a time (independently, at a roughly constant rate) ⇒ the arrival process is a Poisson process, so the interarrival times are exponential (§4.6).
  • Other heuristics
    • Summary statistics: mean, variance, min, max, mode. Also the median (mean ≈ median suggests a symmetric distribution) and the coefficient of variation σ/μ\sigma/\muσ/μ (equal to 1 for the exponential).
    • Histogram (choose an appropriate number of subintervals: too few hides the shape, too many makes it ragged; a common starting point is k≈1+log⁡2nk \approx 1 + \log_2 nk≈1+log2​n, then try a few others). The shape of the histogram estimates the shape of the density.

Q–Q or P–P?

A small change in the tail causes a large gap in the Q–Q plot. In the tails F^\hat FF^ is flat (the density is small), so a tiny difference in probability corresponds to a large difference in xxx; the Q–Q plot compares x-values, so differences in the tails are magnified. In the middle F^\hat FF^ is steep, so a small difference in xxx corresponds to a large difference in probability; the P–P plot compares probabilities, so differences in the middle are magnified. (In the tails both F^\hat FF^ and F~\tilde FF~ are close to 000 or 111, so the P–P points crowd into the corners and tail differences are hidden.)

  • If you want to know whether F^\hat FF^ provides a good approximation of F~\tilde FF~ in the tails → Q–Q plot.
  • If you want to check the middle (the bulk) of the distribution → P–P plot. It is also the one of the two that works for discrete data.

The running example shows both effects on the same data: the Q–Q plot’s biggest gap is in the right tail, the P–P plot’s in the middle.

Goodness-of-fit tests (hypothesis tests)

They test the null hypothesis

H0:the Xi’s are i.i.d. with distribution F^.H_0: \text{the } X_i\text{'s are i.i.d. with distribution } \hat F . H0​:the Xi​’s are i.i.d. with distribution F^.
