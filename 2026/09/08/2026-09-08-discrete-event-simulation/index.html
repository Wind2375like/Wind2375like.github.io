1. Discrete Event Simulation

What a discrete-event simulation consists of — entities, events, the event list, the clock, and statistical counters — walked through on a single-server FCFS queue.

1.1. Stochastic Dynamic Systems

A discrete-event simulation model consists of:

  1. entities — the objects in the system
  2. attributes — properties of an entity
  3. state — variables describing the system at time ttt
  4. events — instants at which the state changes
  5. event list — the scheduled future events, in time order
  6. clock — the current simulated time
  7. statistical counters — running totals used to compute the outputs

1.3. Simulation of an Inventory System (single commodity)

Problem

flowchart RL
    C([customer]) -->|demand| W["warehouse: inventory<br/>(managed by the inventory manager)"]
    W -->|order| SUP([supplier])
    SUP -.->|delivery, after the lead time| W
  • When to order?
  • How much to order?

(Textbook p. 48, §1.5.)

The times between successive demands are i.i.d. Exp(λ)\text{Exp}(\lambda)Exp(λ) with 1/λ=0.11/\lambda = 0.11/λ=0.1 month (on average λ=10\lambda = 10λ=10 demands per month).

The sizes of demands are i.i.d. (and independent of the demand times) with

D={1,w.p. 1/6,2,w.p. 1/3,3,w.p. 1/3,4,w.p. 1/6.D = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{w.p. } 1/6, \\ 2, & \text{w.p. } 1/3, \\ 3, & \text{w.p. } 1/3, \\ 4, & \text{w.p. } 1/6. \end{cases} D=⎩⎨⎧​1,2,3,4,​w.p. 1/6,w.p. 1/3,w.p. 1/3,w.p. 1/6.​

At the beginning of each month, the inventory level is reviewed and a decision is made whether to place an order and how much to order. If an order is placed, the order lead time (delivery time) is uniformly distributed between 0.5 and 1 month, U(0.5,1)U(0.5, 1)U(0.5,1).

Let III = the inventory level at the beginning of a month. The following (s,S)(s, S)(s,S) policy is employed. Let ZZZ be the order amount:

Z={S−I,if I<s,0,if I≥s.Z = \begin{cases} S - I, & \text{if } I < s, \\ 0, & \text{if } I \ge s. \end{cases} Z={S−I,0,​if I<s,if I≥s.​

So sss answers when to order (order as soon as the level at a review is below sss), and SSS answers how much (order up to SSS).

III could be negative (demand backlog): a demand larger than the stock on hand is not lost; the excess is backlogged and filled later. When an order arrives, it first clears the backlog, and the rest goes into inventory.

One can show that for inventory models of this kind (a fixed cost per order, plus per-item ordering, holding and backlog costs) the optimal policy takes this (s,S)(s, S)(s,S) form (Scarf, 1960). It is a theorem for this class of models, not for every inventory system. Here the (s,S)(s, S)(s,S) form is taken as given, and simulation is used to compare different values of sss and SSS.

Cost structure

If an order is placed, the cost is k+c Zk + c\,Zk+cZ (no order, no cost).

kkk is called the setup cost. ccc is the per-unit ordering cost: the price paid for each item ordered, so this part grows with the order size, whereas kkk is paid once per order however many items are ordered (paperwork, transportation). With the textbook’s values k=$32k = \$32k=$32, c=$3c = \$3c=$3, ordering Z=20Z = 20Z=20 items costs 32+3⋅20=$9232 + 3 \cdot 20 = \$9232+3⋅20=$92. Because of kkk, many small orders are expensive, which pushes toward ordering rarely and in large amounts.

There is a holding cost of $h\$h$h per item per month held in inventory (penalizes high inventory levels). Textbook: h=$1h = \$1h=$1.

There is a penalty cost of $p\$p$p per item in backlog per month (penalizes negative inventory levels). Textbook: p=$5p = \$5p=$5 (written π\piπ there).

Simulation

States: inventory level at time ttt, I(t)I(t)I(t); and the amount ZZZ of the outstanding order, if any. (The lead time is at most 1 month and reviews are monthly, so at most one order is outstanding at a time.)

Events: demand arrival, order arrival, inventory evaluation (at the beginning of each month), end of simulation.

Statistical counters: total ordering cost, area under I+(t)I^+(t)I+(t), area under I−(t)I^-(t)I−(t) (defined below).

Let I+(t)=max⁡{I(t),0}I^+(t) = \max\{I(t), 0\}I+(t)=max{I(t),0}: items physically held in inventory at time ttt.
I−(t)=max⁡{−I(t),0}I^-(t) = \max\{-I(t), 0\}I−(t)=max{−I(t),0}: items in backlog at time ttt (demand already received but not yet filled).

Why separate? I(t)=I+(t)−I−(t)I(t) = I^+(t) - I^-(t)I(t)=I+(t)−I−(t), and at any time at most one of the two is non-zero. They are charged at different rates: hhh per item per month on hand, ppp per item per month in backlog. Averaging I(t)I(t)I(t) itself would let the positive and negative parts cancel (10 items in stock for half the time and 10 items short for the other half averages to 000, which would look free), and two different prices could not be applied.
